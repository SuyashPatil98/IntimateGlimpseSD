Answer:
Raft and Paxos achieve the same safety guarantees—both solve distributed consensus—but differ fundamentally in **design philosophy and understandability**.

| Aspect | Raft | Paxos |
|---|---|---|
| **Leadership** | Explicit single leader; all decisions routed through it | Leaderless; any proposer can initiate consensus |
| **Design goal** | Understandability and pedagogical clarity | Theoretical correctness (achieved first) |
| **Decomposition** | Three independent subproblems: leader election, log replication, safety | Monolithic algorithm; harder to reason about independently |
| **Flexibility** | Strong leader simplifies logic but less flexible | More flexible; allows leaderless variants |

The genius of Raft is not a theoretical breakthrough—it's an **engineering and pedagogical** one [[Raft]]. Ongaro & Ousterhout explicitly set out to design a consensus algorithm that was "equivalently safe but fundamentally easier to teach, implement, and reason about" than Paxos.

Raft achieves this by introducing an **explicit leader** who handles all client requests and drives log replication. This centralization eliminates the distributed decision-making complexity of Paxos. Additionally, Raft cleanly separates the problem into three subproblems—leader election, log replication, and safety—each with clear, understandable rules. Paxos, by contrast, bundles these concerns together in a way that makes both the algorithm and its correctness proof harder to follow.

In practice, this clarity has led to Raft's **wide adoption** in systems like etcd, Consul, CockroachDB, TiKV, and Kafka, largely displacing Paxos as the consensus protocol of choice for new systems [[Raft]].
